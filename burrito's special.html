The special interest

The central question is whether Rachel's lifelong interest in people, power, and society qualifies as a special interest in the autistic sense. The interest centers on the mechanics underneath: power dynamics, hierarchy, class, institutional harm, moral responsibility, identity, and how social systems reproduce or suppress. She describes it as something she can't unsee: She describes it as something she can't unsee: "you cannot unsee class struggles, power dynamics, and socioeconomic stratification, it is everywhere."

  • Mechanism over memorization. She wants to understand how systems work and why they fail rather than accumulate facts.
  • Automatic and background. The analysis starts on its own.
  • Broad topics, narrow route. The surface subjects vary but the recurring lens is consistent: who has power, who is harmed, what structure makes this possible, whether it is right or wrong.
  • Early and persistent. She was consuming college-level sociology, economics, and psychology content around age 10, and has long preferred nonfiction, social documentary, and media essays on social implication and symbolism.

Misconceptions Rachel had about special interests

Rachel did not initially believe she had a special interest, and was at one point dismissed by a doctor on those grounds. Several misconceptions shaped that conclusion.

  • SI requires expertise. She assumed a special interest meant being an encyclopedic expert in a narrow domain. Because she doesn't memorize facts or figures, she ruled herself out. History buffs memorize dates; she thinks in mechanisms. She didn't see how that could count.
  • SI requires something unusual. Her interest in people, society, and power felt too broad and too socially legible to qualify. It seemed like something many people cared about, not a distinguishing autistic trait.
  • That ADHD invalidates SI. Because her interests come in bursts and lose fuel after a few weeks, she didn't feel they were sustained enough to be genuine. She described a "graveyard of interested subject matters" and concluded nothing stuck long enough to be significant.
  • That she didn't deserve to belong. She noted explicitly: "I never really thought I deserved to belong." The bar she held herself to was the most stereotypical autistic presentation, and she didn't meet it.

The clinical note in her assessment addresses this directly: "no special interest" is not a valid basis for dismissal

DSM-5 requires at least two of four RRB criteria, and the term is widely misunderstood as requiring encyclopedic expertise. The actual criterion is high-intensity engagement that returns repeatedly and serves a regulatory or meaning-making function. It has little to do with how "special" the interest is, but more to do with how she is navigating through the interest, and how she approach social interactions in relation to this interest.

AuDHD nuance

She's operating under an AuDHD working hypothesis. ADHD hyperfixations tend to be bursty and scattered, which means the depth of engagement fluctuates. The synthesis draws a useful distinction: the subtopics may rotate (fandoms, COVID, games, politics) while the deeper attractor (power, society, harm) remains constant.

Special interest as coping mechanism

The interest serves a regulatory function alongside its intellectual one. Research on monotropism describes autistic special interests as something people literally need during stress and life transitions to stay functional. Rachel's notes show this pattern clearly.

  • Stress activation. The interest intensifies during periods of uncertainty: COVID, moving, new jobs. Rather than going dormant under pressure, it becomes more consuming and more pronounced in a way that her understanding of the macro structure and inequality can drive her spiraling and actions (e.g. not wanting to work because of CAPTITALISM RANT).
  • Autistic inertia example. During a day spent in the autism rabbit hole, she notes being stuck in bed from noon to 7pm, unable to stop. The engagement had its own momentum. And she does not feel like this is something she can't get out of. Rather, she enjoys spending time doing this. But her head really hurt and she needs to stop now.
  • Displaced emotion routing. She also acknowledges a darker edge: some of her most intense political and structural analysis in personal relationships was probably displaced emotion about intimacy and feeling unseen, routed through the only vocabulary available to her. The interest provided a legible frame for feelings she couldn't name directly, consistent with her alexithymia profile.
