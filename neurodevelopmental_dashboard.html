Therapy Fit: Body-Based Approaches

Not all somatic work is equal. The distinction is whether it attends to emotion in the body (which amplifies distress) or uses purely physical regulation.
⚠ Poorly tolerated
Emotion-focused body scans: "notice where you feel the emotion"

Daily Life Impact: Observations by Domain
Social · RAADS-R + CAT-Q

Social effort is invisible from the outside

Running three camouflaging strategies simultaneously (Compensation, Masking, Assimilation) is more cognitively expensive than relying on one. Social situations that look "fine" can be genuinely exhausting. Recovery time after social events is real and necessary.

Interests · ADHD + Autism interaction

A graveyard of hobbies ≠ shallow interests

ADHD generates novelty-seeking; autism generates deep investment in meaning and pattern. The result is high-intensity bursts of engagement, apparent abandonment, and periodic return. The interests are real; the fuel system is impaired, not the interest itself.

Emotions · TAS-20 + MAIA-2

"Something is wrong" without knowing what

Frequently feel something strongly, but the signal doesn't reliably translate into a nameable emotion. The antenna is aimed inward and the motivation is strong; the signal is degraded. This is introspective alexithymia, not emotional disengagement.

Sensory · AASP

Chronic background management effort

Compensatory strategies (closing doors, noise-blocking, managing exposure) work well enough that the sensory burden is invisible to others. The cost compounds over a day or week into significant fatigue, unnoticed by others.

Body · MAIA-2 + AASP

Same regulatory difficulty: inward and outward

The sensory profile (cannot filter external input) and the interoceptive profile (cannot filter internal signals) describe the same underlying pattern at two levels simultaneously. This is one problem, not two separate ones.

Cognition · TestMyBrain

Speed gap feels disorienting in routine environments

Exceptional at depth, pattern recognition, and complex analysis. Below average when tasks require speed, clerical processing, or multistep working memory. The environment often rewards speed over depth, making the mismatch disproportionately visible.

Emotions · Self-report (Part 8)

Anger and excitement are the hardest emotions to identify

Sadness is relatively easy to detect. Anger and excitement (both high-arousal states) are very difficult in the moment, and are typically understood only later through logical analysis of the situation. Emotional processing is retrospective and analytic, not immediate and felt. The complexity is real: "my emotions are very complex, so I don't like to think about them all the time and dissect every layer."

Busting Common AuDHD / ASC Misconceptions
"You can't be autistic if you don't have one encyclopaedic special interest."
This is the exact basis on which a previous clinician dismissed the autism question; it is not a valid criterion. DSM-5 requires at least two of four RRB categories; a single focused interest is only one of them. The other three (repetitive behaviours, insistence on sameness, and sensory reactivity) are all strongly present here. "Special interest" is also widely misunderstood as requiring encyclopaedic expertise in a narrow domain. The actual criterion is high-intensity, recurring engagement that serves a regulatory or meaning-making function. Probable candidates in this profile include psychology, language, social rules, ethics, authentic relationships, and meaning systems — none of which look like a trivia collection, but all of which are driven by the same investment. ADHD further disrupts the appearance: interest is real, the fuel system is impaired.

Questions to Bring to a Clinician

Key lines of inquiry for a formal assessment appointment
  • 1. What early patterns did family or teachers notice? Sensory sensitivities, social confusion, intense interests, difficulty with transitions?
  • 2. After social events, how much recovery time is needed? Is there post-event replaying of conversations?
  • 3. What happens when routines are disrupted? What self-regulatory behaviours are present?
  • 4. Did anxiety and depression emerge independently, or in response to periods of increased masking load?
  • 5. What environments are avoided? What is the recovery time after unavoidable sensory exposure?
